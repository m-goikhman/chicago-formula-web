## Part 1: Three Weeks Later

It is January 8th. Three weeks have passed since the attack on Alex Martin.

The Christmas decorations are coming down across Chicago. The slush on the pavements has been cleared. The university has reopened after the winter break, and somewhere on the North Side, in a room at Northwestern Memorial Hospital, Alex Martin has woken up.

Sergeant Nina Reyes sits at her desk and looks at the evidence bag in front of her.

Tim Kane is in custody. He has been charged with assault and theft. He confessed. The investigation, officially, is over.

But there is still something Nina cannot put aside.

She picks up the bag. Inside is a small USB drive in the shape of a toy aeroplane. It was found in Tim's pocket on the night of his arrest. Tim had struck a man unconscious with a heavy trophy. He had waited outside in the cold for twenty minutes. He had done all of this for this small object.

Nina turns the bag over in her hands. She wants to understand why.

She picks up her phone and dials the university.

## Part 2: The Expert

Dr. James Thornton is a postdoctoral researcher in the Finance Department at the University of Chicago Business School. He is thirty years old, but people often assume he is older — and a professor — because of his formal manner and the quiet authority with which he speaks. He meets Nina in his office, which is small and extremely tidy. Stacks of papers are arranged in perfect order on the shelves.

Nina explains the situation briefly and passes him the USB drive.

Dr. Thornton inserts it into his computer, opens the file, and reads. After less than a minute, he looks up.

"This is nonsense," he says.

Nina waits.

"I don't mean it's a failed attempt," he continues. "I mean it isn't mathematics at all. It is symbols arranged to look like mathematics. Someone wrote this specifically to impress people who don't know the field."

"Can you show me an example?"

He points at the screen. "This part here reads 'expected value of infinity, given maybe.' That is literally a joke. 'Maybe' is not a mathematical variable." He pauses. "And here, the formula uses an operation on an object that cannot be operated on. It's meaningless at every level."

"Who could have written something like this?"

"Someone who has read enough mathematics to imitate the appearance of it. A graduate student could do this. A bright undergraduate could do this." He folds his hands on the desk. "The intention was clearly to deceive. The question, Sergeant, is who they were trying to deceive — and whether they succeeded."

Nina closes her notebook and walks out into the corridor.

One person can answer that question.

## Part 3: The Patient

Alex Martin looks better than Nina expected.

He is sitting up in the hospital bed in a blue and white gown, with a bandage still wrapped around the back of his head. He is pale and a little thinner than his photograph in the case file. But his eyes are clear, and when Nina comes in, he smiles.

"Sergeant Reyes." He holds out his hand. "Thank you for everything. Really."

Nina shakes it and sits down beside the bed.

They talk for twenty minutes. Alex is calm and cooperative. He talks about the evening of December 18th — the party, Tim, the argument with Pauline, the USB drive. He confirms everything the investigation had already established. He asks about Fiona. He asks whether Ronnie is all right.

"He's fine," Nina says.

"I'm glad." Alex looks out of the window. "I know Ronnie and I had a financial arrangement. I know how that looks. But he was always fair to me."

"And Tim?"

Alex is quiet for a moment. "I feel — I don't know. I'm not angry, exactly. I feel sad. He was in trouble and didn't ask for help. He could have just talked to me." He shakes his head slowly. "I didn't know it had gotten that bad for him."

Nina watches him. He is warm and easy to talk to. Everything he says is reasonable. Everything he says matches the facts.

She takes out her notebook.

"Alex," she says. "I've been to the university this morning."

## Part 4: The Revelation

"I've been to the university this morning," Nina says. "I asked someone from the Finance Department to look at the files on the drive."

Alex looks at her. "Oh?"

"He told me the formula isn't real." She keeps her voice neutral. "He said it isn't a failed or incomplete formula. It's nonsense. Symbols put together to look impressive, but with no mathematical meaning whatsoever."

There is a pause. Then Alex's expression shifts — and he laughs.

"Oh," he says. "Oh no. You've got the wrong one."

Nina waits.

"The plane one." Alex seems genuinely amused. He rubs his forehead. "Of course. Every year our department does this — we make fake papers, absurd formulas, ridiculous graphs. Last April I made the most deliberately nonsensical formula I could think of. I even put 'maybe' in the denominator as a joke. The whole department laughed." He looks at Nina. "I saved it on the aeroplane drive. It's been sitting in my desk drawer for nine months."

Nina writes. "And the real formula?"

"It's on a plain drive. Ordinary-looking. Nothing special." He pauses. "I told Pauline to grab 'the plain one.' She must have heard 'the plane one.' She took the joke drive instead of the real one." He shakes his head. "An honest mistake. She couldn't have known."

"So the drive Tim stole —"

"Was worthless." Alex's voice is quiet now. "He hit me over the head for a nine-month-old April Fools joke."

Nina closes her notebook and stands up. She looks at him for a moment.

"Convenient," she says, "that the drive with the real formula turns out to be the one nobody can find."

She says goodbye and walks out into the corridor.

## Grammar Focus: Modal Verbs for Deduction

When we are not certain about facts, we use modal verbs to express what we think is probable, possible, or impossible.

**For present deductions:**
- **must** = I'm almost certain it's true. *He must be hiding something.*
- **might / could** = It's possible. *She might know more than she's saying.*
- **can't** = I'm almost certain it's not true. *This can't be a real formula — it makes no sense.*

**For past deductions:**
- **must have + past participle** = I'm almost certain it happened. *Tim must have known the building code.*
- **might have + past participle** = It's possible it happened. *She might have misheard him.*
- **can't have + past participle** = I'm almost certain it didn't happen. *Alex can't have forgotten — it was too important.*

## Grammar Focus: Contrast and Concession

We use contrast language to show that two ideas are surprising or unexpected together.

**Joining two clauses in one sentence:**
- **although / even though** = used before a clause (subject + verb). *Although Alex seemed cooperative, Nina wasn't sure she believed him.*
- **despite / in spite of** = followed by a noun phrase or verb + -ing. *Despite his explanation, Nina had doubts.*

**Connecting two separate sentences:**
- **However** = at the start of the second sentence, followed by a comma. *Alex seemed cooperative. However, something felt wrong.*

| Word | Followed by |
|------|-------------|
| although / even though | subject + verb |
| despite / in spite of | noun or verb+-ing |
| however | new sentence |
